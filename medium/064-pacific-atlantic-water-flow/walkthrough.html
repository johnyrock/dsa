Pacific Atlantic Water Flow, narrated

Scroll through the thinking, from tracing water downhill from every cell to two uphill searches that start at the oceans and meet in the middle, with the state of the algorithm shown alongside.

Pattern used Graph Traversal reverse the flow: DFS uphill from each ocean's edge, intersect the two visited sets

STEP 1

The problem

A grid of heights is an island. The Pacific touches its top and left edges, the Atlantic its bottom and right edges. Rain on a cell flows to any 4-directional neighbour whose height is less than or equal to the cell's own, and from an edge cell into the ocean on that edge. Return every cell whose water can reach both oceans.

Equal heights count as downhill, so water wanders freely across a plateau. Diagonal moves are not allowed.

Running example: the 5 × 5 grid from LeetCode. The answer is seven cells: [0,4], [1,3], [1,4], [2,2], [3,0], [3,1], [4,0]. Take (2, 2) at height 5: it drains up through 3 then 2 to the top row (Pacific), and down through 1 to the bottom row (Atlantic).

The output is a set of coordinates in any order, not a count. Row-major order is the convention this solution produces.

STEP 2

First instinct: trace the water from every cell

Follow the rules literally. From each cell, flood fill downhill (to neighbours with heights[nr][nc] <= heights[r][c]) and record whether the fill ever touches a Pacific edge and whether it ever touches an Atlantic edge:

for r in range(rows):    for c in range(cols):        reached = flood_downhill(r, c)        if touches_pacific(reached) and touches_atlantic(reached):            out.append([r, c])

Each flood can cover the whole grid, and there are m × n of them: O((m × n)²). On a 200 × 200 plateau every one of the 40,000 floods visits all 40,000 cells.

The waste is that floods from neighbouring cells overlap almost entirely. If (2, 2) drains to the Pacific through (1, 2), then the flood from (1, 2) repeats that exact path, and so does every cell above it.
STEP 3

Reframe: start at the ocean and walk uphill

"Water from A can flow to B" means there is a path from A to B that never steps up. Read the same path backwards from B and it never steps down. So the set of cells that drain into the Pacific is exactly the set of cells reachable from the Pacific's edge by moving only to neighbours that are at least as high.

That is one traversal per ocean, not one per cell. Seed it with every edge cell that touches that ocean, walk uphill, and the visited set at the end is the answer for that ocean.

Two traversals, two visited sets, one intersection. A cell reaches both oceans exactly when both uphill searches reached it.
STEP 4

The key idea: one helper, two seeds, intersect

  • reach(starts) is an iterative DFS. seen = set(starts) and stack = list(starts) begin with every edge cell already visited and queued for expansion.
  • Pop a cell; for each in-bounds neighbour that is not in seen and has heights[nr][nc] >= heights[r][c], add it to seen and push it. Marking on push means no cell is pushed twice.
  • pacific = reach(left column + top row), atlantic = reach(right column + bottom row).
  • Scan the grid row by row and keep every (r, c) that is in both sets.

The corner (0, 0) appears twice in the Pacific seed (once from the column, once from the row). The set absorbs the duplicate, and the second copy on the stack is popped, finds every neighbour already seen, and does nothing.

The panel colours cells blue once the Pacific search has reached them, amber for the Atlantic, and green for both.

STEP 5

Trace it: the Pacific search climbs the top row

The seed puts the left column then the top row on the stack, so (0, 4) is on top. Pop it (height 5): its only in-bounds, unseen neighbour is (1, 4) at height 4, which is lower, so nothing is pushed. Pop (0, 3) (height 3): below is (1, 3) at height 4, and 4 >= 3, so add and push it. Pop (1, 3) (height 4): right is (1, 4) at height 4, equal, so push; below is (2, 3) at height 3, lower, skipped. Pop (1, 4): nothing new.

Pop (0, 2) (height 2): below is (1, 2) at height 3, push. Pop (1, 2): below is (2, 2) at height 5, push. Pop (2, 2) (height 5): every neighbour is lower or already seen, so this branch ends. That is the cell from the problem statement, reached by walking up the same path the water takes down.

STEP 6

Trace it: the Pacific search finishes, the Atlantic search begins

Pop (0, 1) (height 2): below is (1, 1) at height 2, equal, push. Pop (1, 1): below is (2, 1) at height 4, push. Pop (2, 1): below is (3, 1) at height 7, push. Pop (3, 1) (height 7): the tallest cell on the island, and nothing around it is higher, so the branch ends. The remaining stack entries are the left-column seeds, and none of them can climb anywhere new. The Pacific set has 16 cells: rows 0 and 1 in full, then (2, 0), (2, 1), (2, 2), (3, 0), (3, 1), (4, 0).

The Atlantic search starts the same way from the right column and bottom row. Pop (4, 3) (height 2): above is (3, 3) at height 4, push. Pop (4, 2) (height 1): above is (3, 2) at height 1, equal, push. Pop (3, 2) (height 1): above is (2, 2) at height 5 and left is (3, 1) at height 7, push both. Those two cells are now in both sets: the panel turns them green.

STEP 7

Trace it: the intersection

The Atlantic search continues: (4, 0) (height 5) climbs to (3, 0) at height 6; (2, 4) (height 1) climbs to (2, 3) at height 3, which climbs to (1, 3) at height 4. Everything else is a seed or blocked by a lower neighbour. The Atlantic set also has 16 cells: column 4 and row 4 in full, plus (1, 3), (2, 2), (2, 3), (3, 0), (3, 1), (3, 2), (3, 3).

The final scan walks the grid in row-major order and keeps the cells in both sets: (0, 4), (1, 3), (1, 4), (2, 2), (3, 0), (3, 1), (4, 0). Seven green cells, matching the expected output exactly.

Two searches of at most 25 pops each, then one 25-cell scan, instead of 25 separate floods.
STEP 8

Where this goes wrong: the direction of the comparison

Writing heights[nr][nc] <= heights[r][c]. That is the rule from the problem statement, so it looks right, but it is the rule for water flowing away from the current cell. Starting at the ocean and applying it walks downhill into the island, which answers a different question ("which cells can the ocean flood?"). On the running example it returns 15 cells instead of 7, including (0, 0) at height 1, the lowest cell on the island, which cannot reach the Atlantic at all. The panel shows that wrong set: the eleven cells it wrongly includes are struck through, and it also misses three correct ones, (2, 2), (3, 0) and (3, 1).

Using a strict >. Water crosses equal heights, so [[1, 1], [1, 1]] must return all four cells. With > the search never leaves its seeds and every plateau is mis-scored.

Seeding one edge per ocean. The Pacific is the whole top row and the whole left column. Drop the column and (3, 0) at height 6, which reaches the Pacific only by dropping to (2, 0), is lost.

The search runs backwards, so the comparison runs backwards too: from the ocean, step only to neighbours that are at least as high.
STEP 9

Time and space

Each traversal marks a cell in seen the moment it is pushed, so a cell is pushed at most once per ocean and checks four neighbours when popped. Two traversals plus the final scan: O(m × n) time. The two sets and the stack each hold at most every cell: O(m × n) space.

ApproachTimeSpace
Downhill flood from every cellO((m × n)²)O(m × n) per flood
Downhill flood with memoised resultsO(m × n) but hard to get right on plateausO(m × n)
Two uphill searches from the oceansO(m × n)O(m × n)

Memoising the per-cell floods is tempting but cycles through equal heights make the cache unsound mid-computation. Reversing the search avoids the problem instead of patching it.

STEP 10

The final solution

One helper called twice and an intersection. Every line maps to a step above.

  • def reach(starts) is the uphill DFS shared by both oceans.
  • seen = set(starts) and stack = list(starts) treat every edge cell as already reached.
  • (nr, nc) not in seen and heights[nr][nc] >= heights[r][c] is the reversed flow rule with the visited check.
  • seen.add((nr, nc)) before stack.append((nr, nc)) marks on push.
  • pacific = reach(…) seeds the left column and top row; atlantic = reach(…) seeds the right column and bottom row.
  • The list comprehension keeps (r, c) when it is in pacific and (r, c) in atlantic, in row-major order.
One-line summary to remember: search uphill from each ocean's edge, and the cells both searches reach are the ones that drain to both.
