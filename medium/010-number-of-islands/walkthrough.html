Number of Islands, narrated

Scroll through the thinking, from counting land cells to a flood fill that sinks each island the first time it is touched, with the state of the algorithm shown alongside.

Pattern used Graph Traversal scan cells, on unvisited land count one and flood-fill it to water

STEP 1

The problem

You get a grid of '1' (land) and '0' (water). An island is a group of land cells connected up, down, left or right. Count the islands.

Diagonals do not connect. The grid's edge is water. Two blocks that touch only at a corner are two islands.

Running example: a 4 × 5 grid with a 2 × 2 block top-left, a single cell in the middle, and a pair bottom-right. The answer is 3.
STEP 2

First instinct: count the land

The tempting shortcut is to count '1' cells. The example has 7 of them and 3 islands, so that is not it. The question is not "how much land" but "how many separate pieces".

A second instinct is to look at each land cell and ask whether it has a land neighbour above or to the left already counted. That handles simple blobs but fails on shapes like a U, where the two arms are first seen as separate and only join lower down.

Local rules do not work because connectivity is a global property. A cell belongs to whichever island it can reach, and reaching may take a long winding path.
STEP 3

Reframe: the grid is a graph

Every land cell is a node. Every pair of orthogonally adjacent land cells is an edge. An island is a connected component of this graph, and counting islands is counting components.

Counting components has a standard recipe: visit nodes in any order; whenever you meet a node you have not visited, that is a new component, so count it and then traverse everything reachable from it, marking each node visited so it is never counted as the start of another component.

The traversal is a flood fill: depth-first or breadth-first, it does not matter for counting. Both visit exactly the cells of one island.
STEP 4

The key idea: sink the island as you go

Scan the grid row by row. When the scan lands on a '1':

  • Increment the count. This cell is the first touch of a new island.
  • Flood fill from it with an explicit stack. Pop a cell, look at its four neighbours, and for each in-bounds neighbour that is still '1', overwrite it with '0' and push it.

Overwriting to '0' is the visited set. It costs no extra memory, and it guarantees the outer scan will pass over the rest of this island without counting it again. Mark on push, not on pop, so no cell is pushed twice.

An explicit stack rather than recursion matters at the limits: a 300 × 300 grid of all land is one island 90,000 cells deep, well past Python's recursion limit.

STEP 5

Trace it: the first island

The scan starts at (0, 0), which is land. count = 1. Sink it and push it. Pop (0, 0): neighbours (1, 0) and (0, 1) are land, sink both, push both. Pop (0, 1): (1, 1) is land, sink and push. Pop (1, 1): its neighbours are now all water or out of bounds. Pop (1, 0): same. Stack empty.

The whole 2 × 2 block is now '0'. When the scan continues to (0, 1), (1, 0) and (1, 1) it sees water and skips them. The island was counted exactly once.

STEP 6

Trace it: the rest of the grid

The scan crosses water until (2, 2), a lone land cell. count = 2. Sink it; its flood fill finds no land neighbours and ends immediately.

More water, then (3, 3). count = 3. Sink it, push it, pop it, find (3, 4) is land, sink and push, pop it, nothing more. The scan reaches (3, 4), sees water, and finishes.

Every cell was scanned once and every land cell was pushed once. Final answer: 3.
STEP 7

The pitfall: diagonals and bounds

On a checkerboard, every '1' is a separate island because none share an edge. Adding the four diagonal directions to the neighbour list would merge them all into one and return 1 instead of 8.

Four neighbours, not eight. And check 0 <= nr < rows on both sides: in Python, grid[-1] is the last row, so a missing lower-bound check silently wraps around and connects the top edge to the bottom.

One more: the grid holds the string '1'. Comparing to the integer 1 makes every cell look like water and returns 0.

STEP 8

Time and space

The outer scan touches each of the m × n cells once. Across all flood fills, each land cell is sunk and pushed at most once and popped at most once, and each pop examines four neighbours. Total O(m × n) time.

The stack can hold, in the worst case, a large fraction of a single island, so O(m × n) space in the worst case, and no extra memory for the visited set because the grid itself records it.

ApproachTimeExtra space
Recursive DFS, sink in placeO(m × n)O(m × n) call stack, may overflow
Iterative DFS or BFS, sink in placeO(m × n)O(m × n) stack or queue
Either, with a visited setO(m × n)O(m × n) set + stack, grid preserved
STEP 9

The final solution

Sixteen lines. Every one of them maps to a step above.

  • The two for loops are the scan that visits every cell once.
  • if grid[r][c] != "1": continue skips water and already-sunk land.
  • count += 1 fires once per island, on its first cell.
  • grid[r][c] = "0" and stack = [(r, c)] start the flood fill with the seed marked.
  • The while stack loop pops a cell, checks four neighbours with a full bounds test, sinks and pushes each land one.
  • return count.
One-line summary to remember: scan every cell; on unvisited land, count one and flood-fill it away.
