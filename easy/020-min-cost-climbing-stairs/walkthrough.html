Min Cost Climbing Stairs, narrated

Scroll through the thinking, from the exponential recursion to two rolling totals, with the cost of each stair shown alongside.

Pattern used Dynamic Programming To reach a paid stair, arrive from one of the prior two positions and take the cheaper route. Only those two prior costs are needed.

STEP 1

The problem

You are given an array cost where cost[i] is the price of standing on stair i. Once you have paid for a stair you may climb 1 or 2 stairs from it. You may begin on stair 0 or stair 1 for free. The top of the staircase is the position just past the last stair, index len(cost), and standing there costs nothing. Return the minimum total you must pay to reach the top.

Running example: cost = [10, 15, 20]. The top is position 3. Cheapest route: start on stair 1, pay 15, jump two to the top. Answer 15.

Compare the alternatives: start on stair 0, pay 10, jump to stair 2, pay 20, step to the top: 30. Start on 0, pay 10, step to 1, pay 15, jump to the top: 25. Nothing beats 15. Note that the answer is a cost, not a path; you never need to reconstruct which stairs were used.

STEP 2

First instinct: try every path

Define best(i) as the cheapest way to stand on position i. You got there from i - 1 or i - 2, so:

def best(i):    if i <= 1:        return 0    return min(best(i - 1) + cost[i - 1],               best(i - 2) + cost[i - 2])

Call best(3) for the top. It is correct, but each call spawns two more. The panel shows the shape: the top asks stair 2 and stair 1; stair 2 asks stair 1 and stair 0 again. Stair 1 is solved once directly and once inside stair 2's subtree, and on a longer staircase the duplication compounds at every level. That is O(2ⁿ) calls, with the same small subproblems re-solved on every path.

With cost.length up to 1000, this never finishes.

STEP 3

The key insight: a stair only depends on the two below it

The recurrence is right; the recursion is a wasteful way to evaluate it. Ask the narrower question: to know the cheapest total for standing on stair 2, what do I need?

Exactly two numbers: the cheapest total for stair 0 and for stair 1. Stair 2 costs 20 + min(10, 15), its own price plus the cheaper of the two positions you could have jumped from. Nothing further back can matter, because you cannot jump more than 2.

So walk up the staircase instead of recursing down. Compute stair 0's total, then stair 1's, then stair 2's, each from the previous two. That is line 5 of the solution: min(previous, current) + step_cost. And since only the last two totals are ever read, you do not need a full table, just two variables that slide upward.

STEP 4

The approach: two rolling totals

Hold two integers:

  • previous: the cheapest total for the stair two positions back.
  • current: the cheapest total for the stair one position back.

Both start at 0, because you may stand on stair 0 or stair 1 without having paid anything yet; the price of a stair is paid when you step off it, which is why the loop adds step_cost as it leaves.

Each turn of for step_cost in cost computes the next stair's total, min(previous, current) + step_cost, and slides the pair: the old current becomes the new previous. Python's tuple assignment on line 5 does both in one statement, evaluating the whole right side before rebinding either name.

When the loop ends, current is the total for the last stair and previous is the total for the one before it. The top is reachable from either, so the answer is min(previous, current).

STEP 5

Trace it: before the first stair

Line 3 sets previous = 0 and current = 0. Read them as "the cost of having arrived at stair 0" and "the cost of having arrived at stair 1": both are free starting positions.

Nothing has been paid and no stair total is known yet; the panel shows all three stairs as ?. The only thing that exists is the pair of zeros. That is the entire state the algorithm will ever hold, so it is worth pausing on: two integers, not an array of size n.

STEP 6

Trace it: stairs 0 and 1

First turn, step_cost = 10. The new total is min(0, 0) + 10 = 10: to stand on stair 0 and leave it, you pay 10. Slide: previous = 0, current = 10.

Second turn, step_cost = 15. The new total is min(0, 10) + 15 = 15. The min picked 0, meaning "start directly on stair 1" beats "come from stair 0 for 10". Slide: previous = 10, current = 15.

After two turns the pair is previous = 10 (stair 0) and current = 15 (stair 1). The panel shows those two totals filled in and stair 2 still unknown.
STEP 7

Trace it: stair 2 and the top

Third turn, step_cost = 20. The new total is min(10, 15) + 20 = 30: the cheapest way to stand on stair 2 and leave it is via stair 0. Slide: previous = 15, current = 30. The loop is finished.

Now the top. It is one past stair 2, so you reach it either by stepping from stair 2 (total 30) or jumping from stair 1 (total 15). Line 6 returns min(previous, current) = min(15, 30) = 15.

The exponential recursion explored every path to arrive at 15. The loop did three mins and three additions.
STEP 8

Where this goes wrong: returning the last stair

Returning current alone. After the loop current = 30 is the total for stair 2, and it is tempting to write return current. That forces the route through the last stair and reports 30 instead of 15. The top sits after the array, so the final stair can be skipped entirely with a 2-jump from the one before it. The answer needs min(previous, current). The panel marks stair 2's 30 amber and stair 1's 15 green for exactly this reason.

Seeding with the first two costs. Some people start with previous, current = cost[0], cost[1] and loop from index 2. That can be made to work, but it is easy to then add cost[i] one time too many or forget that cost.length may be exactly 2. With the two-zeros seed the loop is uniform: every stair, including 0 and 1, is handled by the same line, and [0, 0] correctly returns 0.

The price of a stair is paid when you leave it, and the top is free. So the loop adds each stair's cost, and the return must consider both of the last two stairs.
STEP 9

Time and space

One pass over cost, doing one min, one addition and one tuple rebinding per stair: O(n) time. Two integers of state regardless of n: O(1) space.

ApproachTimeSpace
Plain recursionO(2ⁿ)O(n) call stack
Recursion + memoizationO(n)O(n) cache + stack
Bottom-up dp arrayO(n)O(n)
Bottom-up, two variablesO(n)O(1)

Memoization removes the repeated subproblems, which is the disastrous part. Rolling two variables instead of a dp array is the final saving, possible because each stair reads only the two totals before it.

STEP 10

The final solution

Four lines of logic, each one a step above.

  • previous, current = 0, 0 seeds the two free starting positions, stair 0 and stair 1.
  • for step_cost in cost visits every stair in order, including the first two.
  • previous, current = current, min(previous, current) + step_cost is the recurrence and the slide in one statement: the new total is this stair's price plus the cheaper of the two positions you could have come from, and the window moves up one.
  • return min(previous, current) because the top is one past the last stair and can be reached from either of the last two.
One-line summary to remember: each stair costs its price plus the cheaper of the two totals below it, and the top is the cheaper of the last two.
